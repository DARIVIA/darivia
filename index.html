The missing expertise

A smart conversationalist is not yet an expert consultant

AI can hold a conversation, explain features, compare products and make a persuasive case for a recommendation. But that is not enough to guide someone consistently toward a confident decision.

The ability to reason about a choice does not mean an app structures its consultation like a professional expert.

An expert uncovers what matters, keeps the reasons for a choice in focus, and helps resolve conflicting priorities. Their recommendation reflects a coherent understanding of the customer and their task – not just the latest message.

A customer profile containing saved attributes, preferences and past outcomes does not, by itself, provide this way of working.

DTI is being built to give AI apps the depth of a highly skilled personal consultant in the luxury segment.

Expert understanding in practice

How understanding the customer changes the recommendation

1. The request

One watch for everyday wear. A budget of around €5,000.

A conventional selection based on size, style and brands produced no options that sparked visual interest.

2. What mattered

Status through design and craftsmanship, without a loud logo. Recognition among connoisseurs, rather than complete obscurity.

Better to wait than settle for a weak compromise.

3. The agent’s response

“Stop searching by silhouette. Look for the material and craftsmanship of the dial.”

“The absence of a strong ‘no’ is not yet a ‘yes’.”

The profile clarified the apparent contradiction: status mattered, but overt branding did not.

The outcome: three alternatives the customer liked, a step-up option, and a recommendation to revisit a variation of the reference model.

Based on a real watch purchase. Context was gathered using the DTI methodology and applied by a specialised AI agent without retraining.

Data and customer control

Relevant context. Controlled access.

The model stays in DARIVIA

DARIVIA creates, stores and develops consumer models from permitted choice events, using DTI. The model itself is not transferred to the host LLM, the brand or the user.

Incoming data is checked for structure, permissions and direct identifiers. Names, contact details, delivery addresses and payment details are excluded from the consumer model.

The profile supports service

The host LLM and user receive a Consumer Profile on request, subject to permissions. This is an interpretation of the model, designed to support personal guidance.

The design includes controls to review and correct the profile, authorise connected apps, revoke access and request deletion. Access to a brand account requires separate authentication with the brand.

Business insights and permissions

Brands receive business interpretations: Consumer Intelligence about their buyers and Market Consumer Intelligence across DARIVIA’s population of anonymised models.

Personalisation, analytics and service improvement, including training the DTI core, are disclosed purposes, subject to the applicable permissions and legal basis. Connecting a profile does not grant access to brand systems.

DARIVIA does not store or reconstruct the full conversation.

Consumer choice analytics

Understand your customers.
Recognise wider consumer patterns.

“It fits the customer” and “the customer is considering buying it” are different outcomes.

Consumer Intelligence explains the needs and choices of your customers: why they consider, choose or reject your products and services.

Market Consumer Intelligence reveals patterns, trends and relationships across DARIVIA’s entire population of anonymised consumer models, translating them into business insights.

Using synthetic consumer models, we explored how customers choose among 680 luxury watches from six brands – revealing shortlist patterns, overlapping product roles and shifts in choice.

1. Assortment coverage

Which needs does the assortment meet, where are the gaps, and which suitable products never reach the shortlist?

2. Product roles

Why does one product win, another become a compromise, and a third lose? Where do products compete for the same place on a shortlist?

3. Response to change

How do changing contexts and priorities reshape the shortlist? Which products retain, lose or gain a place?
